Lynn Conway: The Revolutionizer of Microchips

By Cherielle Team • 01/01/2025

Lynn Conway (January 2, 1983 - June 9, 2024) was an American computer scientist and an electrical engineer. She was also known for her work as a transgender rights activist.

Early Life and Education

As a child, Lynn Conway (previously known as Robert Conway) faced challenges with her gender dysmorphia. As stated in her retrospective, she often felt more inclined towards the feelings and reactions of women rather than men. However, her penchant towards femininity was crushed after an interaction with her mother, who refused to purchase a print dress that Lynn admired. Afterwards, she was restricted from hanging out with her female friends and faced a lack of affection from her parents, who believed that their softness was “causing [her] problem”.

After her parents’ divorce, she lived mainly with her mother. Throughout her childhood, she struggled with suppressing her true identity while discovering different activities in secret that helped her feel more feminine. She had a distant relationship with her mother, who was oblivious to Lynn’s activities as long as she excelled in school.

At 17, she started her undergraduate work at MIT as a physics student. She was brilliant in her studies and was on the High Honor’s Dean List, ranking top 2 percent of the current freshmen. However, in 1959, she left MIT to work as an electronics technician. After several years, she resumed her education at Columbia University, earning a Bachelor of Science (B.S.) degree and a Master of Science in Electrical Engineering (M.S.E.E.) degree.

During this period, Lynn had also met a girl whom she called “Sue”. Sue had become pregnant with Lynn’s child, who they named Kelly. This ultimately resulted in them getting married. Later on, they had a second child named Tracy.

Work Life and Gender Transition

Due to her work on a WISP compiler, Lynn received a job offer from Dr. Schorr. She was invited to his research group at the IBM Research Center. At this new job, she participated in innovating a “superscalar computer”. A superscalar computer has the ability to execute more than one instruction per clock cycle, which paved the way for technology.

As she worked at IBM, she discovered that transitional surgeries were being pioneered. Due to her severe depression, gender dysmorphia, and encouragement from Sue, Lynn sought out these surgeries in order to transition into a female.

While this transition ultimately helped Lynn feel more confident in her own body, there were several drawbacks. She was fired from her job at IBM, leaving her financially unstable. Many colleagues, friends, and extended family had also lost faith in her, feeling ashamed to be seen with her. Additionally, Lynn had lost custody to see her children due to legal constraints, and mutually divorced Sue.

Starting Over

After the transition, Lynn took on a new identity and began to look for work again. She was a contract programmer, digital system designer, and computer architect while “going stealth”. She described the transition as a miracle, finally able to truly be herself and love who she wanted.

In 1971, the Intel 4004, the first microprocessor, caught Lynn’s eye. After she quit her job at Memorex due to a canceled project, she began looking for a job involving the architecture of a microprocessor. Later, she got a job offer from Xerox to do research on special purpose architectures for image processing.

At her job, Lynn met Bert Sutherland, working under him in a group called “LSI Systems Area”. She also worked with Ivan Sutherland and Carver Mead on Introduction to VLSI Systems, a revolutionary piece of work that became a standard in chip design. Later, Bert offered Lynn a chance to teach at MIT. While she had lingering thoughts and feelings about her previous experience there, she ultimately agreed and set for MIT in 1978.

At MIT, Lynn fell back into the old, remembered rhythm that she had experienced years before. Her students began with the projects, such as transformational memory arrays and microprocessors, deeply impressed her.

Afterwards, Lynn returned to her work, creating the concept of scalable design rules and simplified design methods, which streamlined chip designs and design tools. She also created a new form of internet-based infrastructure that encouraged the development of numerous chip designs, leading to many more start-ups in Silicon Valley.

In 1983, Lynn left Xerox to join the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), serving as a key technical architect. She led the Strategic Computing Plan (SCI), one of the government research efforts that utilized technology to prevent Soviet aims during the Cold War.

Later, Lynn joined the University of Michigan as a professor of electrical engineering and computer science. She was also an associate dean of engineering. There, she focused specifically on visual communications, as well as the designing of control systems for internet and user interfaces. She eventually retired in 1999.

In 1987, she eventually met her husband, Charlie. They started off as “canoeing buddies”, but their friendship later blossomed into a quiet romance. They quickly bonded over their mutual interests— he was an electrical engineer and engineering manager. They also both had a love for outdoor activities, which allowed them to bond. When Lynn told Charlie about her past and her transition, she described him as “in a fog for a while”. However, their strong bond allowed them to withstand any hardships and barriers, resulting in a lifelong relationship.

Accomplishments

Over the course of her career, Lynn received a large number of awards. In 1981, she received the Electronics Award for Achievement and a promotion to Xerox Research Fellow. Later, she had also received the Pender Award of the Moore School of the University of Pennsylvania and was elected a Fellow of the IEEE. She, along with Carver Mead, received the Wetherill Medal of the Franklin Institute in 1985.

After joining the University of Michigan, she was honored by election to the National Academy of Engineering, as well as receiving both the National Achievement Award of the Society of Women Engineers and an honorary doctorate from Trinity College. After stepping down from active teaching in 1999, she was awarded the status of Professor of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, Emerita by the Regents of the University of Michigan. Emerita is an honorary title given to an esteemed professor that has retired but is still allowed to preserve their title.

With these awards, along with many more that she received, she felt gratified that she had proven herself despite earlier conflicts in her life. It gave her a sense that she had achieved significant and influential accomplishments, which helped her serve in many important boards and committees later on.

Transgender Rights Activism

In addition to her many accomplishments and breakthroughs in the STEM field, Lynn was also a transgender rights activist. She created a website with information and support, as well as linking several resources for those facing gender dysmorphia or backlash from transitioning. Her belief was that if she spread her story about her transition, others would see this as inspiration and self-motivation. She was also interviewed by GenderTalk Radio, where she had the chance to discuss her personal story and answer questions.

Through her activities, she advocated for the equal rights of transgender people, demanding equal opportunities and employment. As a result of her efforts, she was named one of Stonewall’s 40 trans heroes and was in the article, “25 Transgender People Who Influenced American Culture” by TIME.

Impact

One of Lynn Conway’s most notable achievements is the pathway towards modern microchips. This achievement is especially important because the microchips are found in almost all high-tech systems, including computers, phones, and the Internet. Microchips are essential for technology because it allows different parts of a device to communicate with each other. They are comparable to the neurons of the brain, instructing the device with on and off signals. Without microchips, the majority of devices we use today would be rendered useless.

Lynn was also an important figure of transgender rights activism. Her work and effort paved the way for equal rights today. Through her website, different interviews, and active participation in the transgender community, she was able to improve and spread awareness. Her actions helped inspire others that felt unheard or unseen, giving them the courage to take action to come out and align with their gender identity.
